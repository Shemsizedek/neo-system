# The Tape Doesn't Lie

In acting, the self-tape is the honest mirror.

On set there's a director, a crew, a room full of people reacting to you. In a self-tape there's none of that. Just the camera, the reader on speaker, and you — running the scene, hitting stop, and watching yourself back. No one to impress. No one to blame.

That's what makes it the best teacher I have. You can't hide in a self-tape. The take where you were "fine" turns out to be the take where you rushed the middle. The line you thought landed lands nowhere. The tape doesn't argue with you — it just shows you. And the choice is always the same: watch it and learn, or skip the review and waste the whole exercise.

I picked that habit up on the acting side of my life — the Casting Networks and Backstage circuit, where the self-tape often is the audition. But I've started running the rule everywhere: the only feedback that counts is the kind you can't talk yourself out of.

In the operator's corner, that's the number on the page, not the number in the conversation. In the notebook, that's the checkmark you actually earned, not the one you feel like you earned. In the week itself, it's the five o'clock question — did I keep the hour, or just intend to? The tape only works if you hit play. Most of us skip the review and wonder why the pattern repeats.

So here's the discipline: record, review, repeat. Tape the week. Watch it back. Keep what works, reshoot what doesn't.

What's one part of your week that would get better if you just watched it back honestly?

— Lawiy
