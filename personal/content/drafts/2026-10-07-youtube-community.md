# Be Easy to Direct

Ask any director who they'd rather work with, and the answer is rarely the most talented actor in the room. It's the one who's easiest to direct.

I picked this up on the acting side of my life — the Casting Networks and Backstage grind, where the goal is simply to book the next room. For a while I thought the job was to be impressive. Show range. Have choices. Walk in with the scene fully decided. But a director doesn't need impressive. A director needs movable.

Here's what I mean. A director gives you an adjustment — slower, don't let him see it. The actor who nods, locks the change in on the next take, and hands it back clean — that's the one the whole room exhales around. The actor who argues for their choice, or needs three takes to find the note, or quietly does it their way anyway — the room tenses. Both might be talented. Only one gets the second call.

Taking direction is a skill, not a weakness. It means you prepared enough to hold your choices loosely. It means your ego isn't in the room when the work starts. It means you trust that the person in the chair might see something you can't see from inside the scene.

And here's the part I didn't expect: it transfers. The same muscle works everywhere. I run my week on systems — the gathering at five, the study block, the notebook — because the system is the director and I'm the actor. The plan says one thing, my mood says another, and the system wins. Be easy to direct, even by your own design.

The ego wants to be brilliant. The craft rewards the ones who listen.

So tonight I carry that into the room at five: less arguing with the plan, more receiving it. What's one adjustment you've been resisting that might be the direction you needed all along?
